# Product sense

- **Status:** accepted · **Verified:** 2026-10-31

## Who this is for

Teachers and the students in their classes, and young people who find the page on their own. It has to be exciting enough
to hold them. Assume a reader with no astronomy background and a teacher with a few minutes to prepare, projecting from an
ordinary school laptop.

The audience is a decision. The details below it are working assumptions, not findings, because no
teacher has used this yet. Treat each as a question to test:

- Students are roughly middle-school age and older.
- Teachers will not create accounts, so everything works without one.
- A shared link is how a teacher hands a class the same view.

## What good looks like

1. **A teacher can trust it, and show why.** Accuracy is the product, so the page says where its numbers
   come from and how they were checked.
2. **A student learns something true from the first screen.** The surprise of real scale, that the
   planets are invisible specks, is the lesson, so it is explained and not hidden.
3. **It holds still when a class needs it to.** A link names one moment, and everyone sees the same sky.
4. **Failure is honest.** If the page cannot show correct numbers, it says so in plain words and shows
   none. A picture of the wrong sky is the worst outcome available.
5. **The numbers never depend on the picture.** Every position is in a table that a screen reader or an
   old laptop can use, whether or not WebGL starts.

## What this is not

It may look as exciting as a space game, but it does not trade accuracy for spectacle. Enlarging planets is acceptable only as
a clearly labeled view over true positions, never as the default, and compressing distances is not acceptable at all.
