Solar System Classroom

Where these numbers come from

Every position on this site comes from DE440, the planetary model that NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) publishes. JPL fits the model to decades of radar, spacecraft and telescope measurements, then uses it to navigate missions.

How your browser gets a position

  1. JPL stores each planet's path as a series of polynomials, each covering a few days or weeks.
  2. Our server copies the polynomials for one calendar year into a small file, about 100 kilobytes, without changing them.
  3. Your browser downloads that file and evaluates the polynomials at the current time. Nothing is estimated or rounded along the way.

How the numbers were checked

A file reaches this site only after it agrees with JPL Horizons, the calculator JPL runs for the public, to within 1 kilometer. We checked twelve moments in every year from 2000 through 2050, and the largest difference we measured was about 5 meters.

As a check that does not rely on Horizons, the same file predicts the total solar eclipse of August 12, 2026 at the time and distance that eclipse catalogs published.

How the motion and the lines are calculated

When time runs faster, nothing is animated or estimated. Every frame asks the same data where each planet is on that date, so the motion you see is the real motion, only faster. The data covers 2000 through 2050, which is long enough to watch Saturn go around almost twice, but only about a third of Neptune's 165-year orbit.

Each line is the ellipse a planet would follow if only the Sun pulled on it, worked out from where the planet is and how fast it is moving at the moment shown. The other planets pull on it too, so its real path slowly drifts from that ellipse. We measured the drift against JPL's data: over one full orbit it is largest for Uranus, at about 0.14 astronomical units, which is under 1% of its distance from the Sun. The line is redrawn as time runs, so a planet always sits on its line.

What the page does not show

  • Planet sizes, unless you ask. At true scale, Earth would be far smaller than one pixel with the whole solar system in view, so each dot marks a position and says nothing about size. A separate view enlarges the planets and the Sun, and a label on the picture says by how much.
  • The real stars. The stars behind the planets are decoration, in random positions.
  • The exact center of Mars through Neptune. For those planets the data gives the center of mass of the planet and its moons. That point sits at most a few hundred kilometers from the planet's center, while the last digit in our table stands for about 150,000 kilometers.
  • Moons and spin. These are not on the page yet.
  • Dates outside 2000 through 2050. This site has no planet data for them yet.
