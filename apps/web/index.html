What this page does

This page shows where the Sun and the eight planets are at this moment, at their true distances from one another. It is a calculation, not an artist's picture. The diagrams in most textbooks squeeze the planets together so that they fit on a page, which makes the solar system look crowded. Here every distance is real, and you can see how empty the solar system actually is.

You can use it to:

  • see where every planet is right now, in a three-dimensional view you can turn and zoom;
  • watch the planets travel along their paths, with time running as fast as a year per second;
  • read how far each planet is from the Sun and from Earth, and how long its light takes to reach us;
  • open any moment from 2000 through 2050 and share it as a link, so that a whole class looks at the same positions.

It is written for teachers and students, and it assumes no knowledge of astronomy. There is nothing to install, and you do not need an account.

How to use it

Turn and zoom the picture

Drag the picture to turn it. Scroll, or pinch on a touch screen, to zoom in and out. The view always circles the center of the solar system, so you cannot lose your way by sliding off to one side.

Under What to show, "All eight planets" frames the whole solar system. "Inner planets, Mercury to Mars" moves the camera closer, until those four are far enough apart to tell from one another. Neither choice changes a distance: only your viewpoint moves.

Each dot marks where a planet is, and a dot's size says nothing about how big the planet is. Each line is the path that planet is following around the Sun. When labels would overlap, some stay hidden until you zoom in.

Make time run

Under How fast time runs, choose one of these:

Right now
The page follows your computer's clock, and the planets look still. That is true to life. Earth travels about 30 kilometers every second, but with all eight planets in view one pixel covers about 18 million kilometers, so Earth needs about a week to cross a single pixel.
Stopped
The date holds still, and a link to that moment appears.
1 day per second
Mercury goes around in about 88 seconds. Try it with the inner planets in view.
1 week per second
Mercury goes around in about 13 seconds and Earth in about 52 seconds.
1 month per second
Earth goes around in about 12 seconds and Mars in about 23 seconds.
1 year per second
Jupiter goes around in about 12 seconds and Saturn in about 29 seconds, while the inner planets race around too fast to follow.

Time runs forward only, for now, and it stops at the end of the data. The page tells you when that happens.

Show a class one moment

Choose Stopped, then share the link that appears. Anyone who opens it sees the same positions and the same numbers, whatever their time zone.

You can also write such a link yourself, by adding ?at= and a date to the page's address. For example, ?at=2026-08-12T17:46:00Z opens 17:46 Coordinated Universal Time on August 12, 2026, the minute the total solar eclipse of that day is at its greatest. The Z at the end means Coordinated Universal Time. A date on its own, such as ?at=2026-08-12, means midnight at the start of that day in the same time scale.

Read the table

The table gives the same positions as numbers, for the moment shown above it, and it follows the picture as time runs. A distance is the straight line through space between two bodies at that moment, in astronomical units (AU), a measure explained below. Distances are rounded to three decimal places, so the last digit stands for about 150,000 kilometers. The last column divides each planet's distance from Earth by the speed of light.

Use it with a keyboard or a screen reader

The speed and view controls are ordinary radio buttons: press Tab to reach a group, and the arrow keys to change the choice. The table holds every number behind the picture, and it stays on the page even if a computer cannot start the 3D view. The picture itself cannot yet be turned or zoomed from the keyboard, which is a known gap.

Things to try

  1. Watch Mercury hurry. Choose the inner planets and 1 week per second. Mercury speeds up as it swings close to the Sun, then slows as it climbs away. Kepler's laws, below, explain why.
  2. Time the laps. At 1 month per second, count how many seconds Earth and Mars take to go once around. Then compare your counts with the table of orbit sizes below.
  3. Follow a message to Mars. Run time at 1 week per second and watch the last column of Mars's row. Between 2000 and 2050, light or a radio signal takes from about 3 minutes to about 22 minutes to cross the gap. Why does that mean nobody can steer a Mars rover with a joystick?
  4. See how slowly the outer planets move. Open January 1, 2000 and choose 1 year per second. By the end of 2050, Saturn has gone around 1.7 times, and Neptune has not yet gone a third of the way.
  5. Turn the solar system on edge. Drag until you are looking along the plane of Earth's orbit. Earth's line flattens to a straight stroke, and Mercury's stands out at a tilt of 7 degrees.
  6. Walk it outdoors. Suppose the Sun were a ball 10 centimeters across. Earth would be a 1-millimeter bead 11 meters away, Jupiter a 1-centimeter marble 56 meters away, and Neptune a 4-millimeter bead about 325 meters away. The nearest other star would be about 2,900 kilometers away.

How it works

You might expect a page like this to keep asking a server where the planets are. It does not, because a planet's position is not news. JPL has already calculated the paths for many years ahead, so the page downloads JPL's formulas and does the arithmetic itself. Here is the chain from JPL to your screen.

  1. JPL publishes the paths. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) publishes a model of the solar system called DE440. It stores each planet's path as a long chain of polynomials: short formulas, each good for a few days or weeks.
  2. A small program copies out one year at a time. This site's program downloads JPL's file and confirms that it is the exact file expected. It then copies each year's polynomials into a file of about 100 kilobytes, without changing a digit.
  3. The program checks each year against JPL. It asks JPL Horizons, a calculator JPL runs for the public, where the eight planets and the Moon are at twelve moments in the year. If any answer differs from the file by more than 1 kilometer, that year is rejected and never reaches this site.
  4. Your browser downloads the year it needs. Before reading a single number, it compares the file with a digital fingerprint recorded when the file was published. If they differ, the page says the data arrived damaged and shows no numbers, because a picture of the wrong sky is worse than none.
  5. Your computer does the arithmetic. Every time it draws the picture, your browser works out each planet's position from the polynomials. No server is asked and nothing is filled in between frames, so even at a year per second, each frame shows where JPL's data puts the planets on that date.
  6. The next year arrives ahead of time. While time runs, the browser fetches the next year's file before it is needed, so that the motion does not stall at New Year.

All of the code is open source, so anyone can read how this works or check the numbers: the project on GitHub.

The science behind the page

Where a planet's position comes from

Nobody reads these positions off a telescope. JPL calculates them. Its model, DE440, starts from the laws of gravity and steps the whole solar system forward through time, adding up the pull on each body from the Sun, the planets, the Moon, 343 asteroids, 30 large objects beyond Neptune, and a ring standing in for the rest of the Kuiper belt out there.

A calculation like that is only as good as its starting point, so JPL adjusts the starting positions and masses until the results fit decades of measurements. Those include radio signals timed to and from spacecraft such as MESSENGER at Mercury, Venus Express, the orbiters at Mars, Juno at Jupiter and Cassini at Saturn, as well as lasers bounced off reflectors on the Moon. The fit is remarkably close: DE440's distances to MESSENGER differ from the radio measurements by only about 0.7 meters, typically.

Why orbits are ellipses: Kepler's laws

Early in the 1600s, Johannes Kepler found three rules in the planets' motion. Later that century, Isaac Newton showed that all three follow from one idea: gravity weakens with the square of distance. You can see each of them on this page.

  1. Each orbit is an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus. An ellipse is a stretched circle, and its eccentricity measures the stretch, where 0 is a perfect circle. Most planets' orbits are so nearly circles that they look round here. Mercury's, at about 0.21, does not.
  2. A planet moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun. A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so the planet must speed up where the line is short. Mercury ranges from about 39 to about 59 kilometers per second.
  3. The farther out a planet is, the longer its year, by an exact rule. Take its average distance a, the midpoint of its closest and farthest distances from the Sun, in AU. Take the time T for one orbit, in years. Then T squared equals a cubed, as the last two columns of the second table show.

What the lines really are

Each line is an osculating orbit, from the Latin word for kissing: the ellipse that touches a planet's real path at the moment shown. The page takes the planet's position and velocity at that moment, adds the pull of the Sun, and works out the ellipse the planet would follow if nothing else pulled on it.

Something else always does pull on it, so the real path slowly drifts away from any one ellipse. Over a year the drift is at most 0.0004 AU, or about 60,000 kilometers, far less than a pixel. Over a full orbit it is largest for Uranus, at about 0.14 AU, which is under 1% of its distance from the Sun. The page redraws each line every 30 simulated days, so a planet stays on its line as time runs. The dots come straight from JPL's data; the lines are a guide for the eye.

Measuring in astronomical units and light

One astronomical unit (AU) is exactly 149,597,870.7 kilometers, a value the International Astronomical Union fixed in 2012, close to Earth's average distance from the Sun. Earth's orbit is an ellipse, so its real distance swings between 0.983 and 1.017 AU during the year.

Light travels exactly 299,792.458 kilometers every second. So sunlight takes between 8.2 and 8.5 minutes to reach Earth, and Neptune's light takes about four hours. You always see the sky as it was, and the table's last column says how long ago. The table divides the distance at the moment shown by the speed of light, without allowing for how far the planets move during the trip.

Keeping time

Your computer's clock keeps Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), which now and then gains a leap second to stay in step with Earth's slightly uneven spin. The planets' motion has no use for leap seconds, so JPL's calculations use a smooth time scale called Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB). In 2026, TDB runs about 69 seconds ahead of UTC. That sounds like nothing, yet Earth travels about 2,000 kilometers in 69 seconds, so the page converts every moment before it asks where a planet is.

Where the center is

The Sun is not quite the center of the solar system. The Sun and the planets all orbit their shared center of mass, the barycenter, and the giant planets, above all Jupiter, keep moving it. Between 2000 and 2050 it lies anywhere from about 93,000 to about 1.38 million kilometers from the center of the Sun. The Sun's radius is about 696,000 kilometers, so the point the Sun circles sometimes sits outside the Sun altogether.

The picture is centered on this barycenter, as JPL's data is, so if you zoom all the way in with time running, you can watch the Sun's dot drift. The table measures from the Sun's center. Earth and the Moon do the same thing on a smaller scale: Earth's center circles their shared center of mass each month, at a distance of about 4,700 kilometers. Earth's line follows that point, so it does not wobble.

Which way is up

JPL's coordinates are anchored to quasars so distant that their directions do not measurably change, in a system called the International Celestial Reference Frame. Its axes lie close to Earth's equator. This page turns them by 23.44 degrees, the angle between Earth's equator and its orbit, so that Earth's orbit lies flat and up on the screen is the north side of that plane.

How a few numbers hold a whole orbit

Storing a position for every instant would take endless numbers. So JPL cuts each path into short spans and fits one polynomial per axis to each span, using a family called Chebyshev polynomials. A fast, tightly curving path needs more numbers: Mercury gets 14 per axis every 8 days. Neptune, moving slowly along a wide curve, needs only 6 per axis every 32 days. A whole year of the solar system comes to about 13,000 numbers.

The math, for the curious

A position along one axis at time t, inside a span that starts at t0 and lasts Δt, uses the coefficients ck stored for that span:

x(t)= ∑k=0n−1 ckTk(s) , s= 2(t−t0)Δt −1

The Chebyshev polynomials build on one another, and your browser evaluates the sum with Clenshaw's recurrence, which is fast and resists rounding error:

T0=1, T1=s, Tk+1= 2sTk−Tk−1

An orbit line comes from the position r and velocity v relative to the Sun, and μ, the combined gravity of the Sun and the planet. These give the orbit's size a and its distance from the Sun at each angle θ past the closest point:

1a= 2|r|− |v|2μ , ρ(θ)= h2/μ1+ecosθ

Here h is the size of the cross product of r and v, and e is the eccentricity.

One practical problem remains. A graphics card stores positions as 32-bit numbers, which near Neptune can tell apart only points at least 512 kilometers from each other. So the page subtracts the camera's position while the numbers are still 64-bit, and hands the card only small distances from the camera.

How we know the numbers are right

  • Against JPL Horizons. Every year's file was compared with Horizons at 12 moments, for 9 bodies, across 51 years: 5,508 comparisons. The largest difference was 4.7 meters. Horizons runs DE441, a sister model to DE440, so this is a comparison with a separate calculation and not with the same file.
  • Against a real eclipse. NASA's eclipse catalog gives the total solar eclipse of August 12, 2026 its greatest moment at 17:47:06 Dynamical Time, with the axis of the Moon's shadow passing 0.8977 Earth radii from Earth's center. Worked out from this site's file for 2026, the same moment comes out within a second, and the same distance within 0.0002 Earth radii. Neither figure comes from Horizons.
  • Against NASA's published orbits. The size, stretch and tilt of each orbit, and the top and bottom speeds of Mercury and Earth, agree with NASA's fact sheets. Mercury's speed, for example, comes out between 38.86 and 58.98 kilometers per second, against NASA's 38.86 and 58.97.

These checks show that this site copies and evaluates JPL's data faithfully. They do not measure how close JPL's model is to the real planets. For that, read the DE440 paper listed under Sources.

What this page leaves out

  • Planet sizes. With all eight planets in view, one pixel covers about 18 million kilometers. The Sun is 1.4 million kilometers across, so at true scale even the Sun would cover less than a tenth of a pixel, and Earth about a thousandth. That is why each dot marks a position and says nothing about size.
  • Where to look in your own sky. The page shows where the planets are in space, not where to point a telescope. That would need your location and a correction for the time light takes to arrive.
  • The exact center of Mars through Neptune. For those planets the data gives the center of mass of the planet and its moons, at most a few hundred kilometers from the planet's center. The last digit in the table stands for about 150,000 kilometers.
  • Moons, spin, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets. These are not on the page yet.
  • Dates outside 2000 through 2050, and running time backward. Uranus and Neptune therefore cannot finish an orbit here, since they take 84 and 165 years.
